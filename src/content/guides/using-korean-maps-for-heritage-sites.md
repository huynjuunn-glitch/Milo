A heritage-site map pin can answer “where is the property?” while failing to answer “where can a visitor enter?” Palaces have several gates, temple compounds stretch up mountainsides, fortress names cover long walls, and an archaeological zone may include roads, museums and closed ground.

The solution is not loyalty to one app. It is a repeatable chain: **identify the official place, match a local record, verify the usable entrance, test the transit chain, and save a recovery card outside the live map**.

This guide describes functions rather than promising exact button labels. Map interfaces, languages and routing features change; recheck the provider's current help page before publication or travel.

The [Korea Heritage Service Heritage Search](https://english.cha.go.kr/chaen/search/selectGeneralSearchList.do?mn=EN_02_02) is a useful identity source for designated heritage. It can provide the official name, classification, region and manager. For operating hours and gates, continue to the current site operator; the heritage record and visitor operation page perform different jobs.

## Choose a local map without turning it into authority

NAVER describes NAVER Map as a Korea-wide service for discovering and saving places and finding routes across transport modes ([NAVER Corp service page](https://navercorp.com/en/service/map)). Its official help currently documents Korean, English, Simplified Chinese and Japanese language options in supported app versions ([NAVER Map Help](https://help.naver.com/service/5637/contents/8275?osType=MOBILE)).

Kakao describes KakaoMap as offering routes for public transit, car, walking and cycling in South Korea ([Kakao service page](https://www.kakaocorp.com/page/service/service/kakaomap?lang=en)). The Seoul Metropolitan Government also points visitors to NAVER Map and KakaoMap for route recommendations and live bus tracking in the capital, while listing Seoul's own transport information as an alternative ([Seoul public transportation guide](https://english.seoul.go.kr/service/movement/public-transportation/)).

These sources establish useful capabilities. They do **not** establish that every place record, translated label, path or business hour is correct. Use at least one local map for routing, then cross-check the visitor entrance with the operator.

## Search in a disambiguating order

Try the most specific input first:

1. Hangul site name plus city or district;
2. complete Korean road address;
3. Hangul gate, visitor center, museum or ticket-office name;
4. English name only as a fallback.

Open the candidate record and match at least two fields. A name plus region is the minimum; a matching address and official website provide stronger evidence. Watch for records that represent a restaurant, parking lot, Templestay office or similarly named temple rather than the heritage destination.

Keep the exact Hangul text in your notes. It can be shown to station staff, a taxi driver or a visitor-information desk even if your phone language changes.

## Replace the property pin with an entrance pin

Large sites require a second search. Look at the operator's “Directions,” “Visitor Information” or reservation page for a named gate, check-in point or ticket office. Then locate that feature on the map.

Ask four questions:

- Does the walking route meet a public gate rather than a wall or property boundary?
- Is ticketing or reservation check-in somewhere else?
- Does the chosen entrance operate on the visit date?
- Will you exit through the same gate?

A central map marker may be geographically accurate and operationally useless. A parking pin may lead pedestrians to a vehicle entrance. A temple program office may not be the casual visitor route. Save both the official property and the practical entrance, labeling them clearly in your own notes.

Do not infer access from aerial imagery, an open-looking road or a user photo. Physical barriers and site rules are newer and more authoritative.

## Expand transit into a chain you can audit

Do not save “Hotel to Temple — 82 minutes” as the plan. Record:

- departure station or stop and travel direction;
- route or line identifier;
- transfer station and walking distance inside it;
- final stop in English and Hangul;
- walk from that stop to the verified entrance;
- return departure point and an earlier alternative.

Check the route close to departure. Live bus and transit information can improve decisions, but it is not a reservation and does not remove the need for a buffer. Rural or mountain connections deserve more time than a frequent urban subway.

If the route uses intercity rail or bus, verify and purchase through the official operator named by a government or tourism source. A map is for orientation; it should not silently replace the booking authority.

## Inspect the final walking segment

Zooming in is not verification by itself. Compare the route with official access text and look for named roads, gates and visitor facilities. Determine whether the last segment is:

- city pavement;
- stairs or underground passage;
- gravel or uneven heritage ground;
- hillside road;
- marked trail;
- shuttle-dependent connection.

If a route line appears to cut across forest, a closed precinct or steep terrain, reject it until an official source confirms public access. Distance alone does not describe effort or safety. Travelers with step-free requirements should ask the operator which entrance and internal route are currently usable.

## Design the return before accepting the outbound route

For every remote site, search the route in reverse. The nearest outbound stop may be served from the other side of the road on return, or the walk back may begin at another exit. Save the Hangul stop name, direction and one earlier service.

Set a departure trigger—for example, “leave the upper precinct by 15:20”—based on the verified connection and walking buffer. If the outbound trip arrives late, remove an optional stop instead of consuming the return margin.

## Create an offline recovery card

A useful offline note is readable without opening any map account. Include:

```text
SITE: [official English name]
KOREAN: [Hangul name]
ENTRANCE: [Hangul gate/check-in name]
ADDRESS: [Korean road address]
OUTBOUND: [line/bus] → [final stop in Hangul] → [walking landmark]
RETURN: [stop in Hangul / direction / earlier option]
OPERATOR: [official URL or published phone]
BASE: [accommodation name and Korean address]
```

Add screenshots that show the route direction and stop context, not just a blue line. Keep the text note available separately in case screenshots are cropped, the app signs out or mobile data is weak.

## Know what each disagreement means

When two sources disagree, classify the disputed field:

- **Heritage name or designation:** prefer the Korea Heritage Service or relevant official register.
- **Opening or gate status:** prefer the current operator notice and on-site sign.
- **Bus or subway operation:** prefer the transport operator or authority, using the map for live orientation.
- **Walking access inside the site:** prefer operator instructions and physical barriers.
- **Religious conduct:** prefer the temple's signs and staff.

Do not average conflicting times or choose the result that makes the itinerary possible. Preserve the disagreement in your note and plan around the more restrictive verified condition until it is resolved.

## Final two-minute test

Before departure, you should be able to answer without searching again:

1. What is the destination in Hangul?
2. Which exact gate or check-in point am I using?
3. What is the final stop and travel direction?
4. What surface connects the stop to the entrance?
5. How and when do I return?
6. What official source overrides the map if conditions change?

If one answer is missing, the route is not finished. Fix the identity or entrance first; adding more pins will not repair it.
