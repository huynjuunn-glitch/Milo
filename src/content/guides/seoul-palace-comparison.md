## Quick recommendation

If you have never visited a Korean palace, choose **Gyeongbokgung** for the clearest formal sequence or **Changdeokgung** for a palace shaped by terrain and long royal use. Choose **Changgyeonggung** for residential and East Palace history, **Deoksugung** for the Korean Empire, and **Gyeonghuigung** for a shorter encounter with loss and reconstruction. Visit **Jongmyo** only when ancestral ritual—not another royal residence—is the subject you want.

Check the day before deciding. The current [official hours and closure table](https://royal.khs.go.kr/ROYAL/contents/R702000000.do) places Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung and Deoksugung on a Monday closure pattern, while Gyeongbokgung and Jongmyo regularly close on Tuesday. Public holidays can shift the following closure. A beautiful itinerary built around the wrong weekday has no value.

Ticket details can change. The [official admission page](https://royal.khs.go.kr/ROYAL/contents/R703000000.do) is the authority for individual and integrated tickets and states that Changdeokgung's Secret Garden requires its own admission in addition to the palace area.

## Gyeongbokgung: best for a formal first reading

Gyeongbokgung was founded in 1395 as Joseon's primary palace. Its ceremonial approach, principal throne hall, working courts and residential compounds allow a first-time visitor to read authority as a sequence. The site is also an unusually direct lesson in reconstruction: the palace burned in 1592, was rebuilt in 1867, suffered systematic dismantling under colonial rule and has been the subject of major restoration since the 1990s. The [Royal Palaces and Tombs Center's history](https://royal.khs.go.kr/ENG/contents/E101010000.do) gives the necessary chronology.

Choose it if scale and political symbolism are your priorities. Avoid it if the group has very limited walking capacity, severe heat is expected or only a short indoor visit will work. Our [Gyeongbokgung first-visit field guide](/guides/gyeongbokgung-first-visit/) provides a route that can be cut to 90 minutes without losing the main argument.

## Changdeokgung: best for landscape and lived royal history

Changdeokgung began in 1405 as a secondary palace, but it was rebuilt before the other palaces after the sixteenth-century invasions and served as the main royal residence for roughly 270 years before Gyeongbokgung's nineteenth-century reconstruction. The [official historical account](https://royal.khs.go.kr/ENG/contents/E102010000.do) also explains its connection with Changgyeonggung as the East Palace.

UNESCO emphasizes the complex's adaptation to uneven topography and the integration of official, residential and garden areas. Read the [World Heritage statement](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/816/) before visiting if “harmony with nature” feels like vague promotional language: it specifies the relationship between layout, terrain, buildings and the rear garden.

The planning trap is the Secret Garden. It is a limited, timed visit with a separate ticket and language schedule. Do not select Changdeokgung solely for the garden and then arrive without checking availability. The main palace remains meaningful without it, but it answers a narrower architectural question.

## Changgyeonggung: best for the royal household and historical disruption

Changgyeonggung grew from a residence associated with an abdicated king and was expanded in 1483 for senior royal women. It functioned with Changdeokgung as the East Palace and was used substantially for royal family life. That makes it a useful choice after learning the public ceremonial sequence elsewhere.

Its story also includes deliberate degradation during Japanese rule: a zoo and botanical garden were installed, the palace was renamed as a park, and restoration began after its name was reinstated in 1983. The [official Changgyeonggung history](https://royal.khs.go.kr/ENG/contents/E103010000.do) makes that disruption central rather than incidental.

Choose Changgyeonggung for a paired East Palace day or an evening-capable schedule under the current opening table. Do not treat the surviving greenhouse or landscape as proof that the colonial park was harmless; the conversion changed the status and fabric of a royal site.

## Deoksugung: best for the Korean Empire

Deoksugung's earlier name was Gyeongungung. It became the imperial palace when Gojong proclaimed the Korean Empire in 1897. Traditional halls and Western-style buildings therefore belong to a political project of imperial court-making, not to an eccentric design collection. Fire, Japanese pressure, colonial reduction and later restoration also shaped the site. The [official Deoksugung chronology](https://royal.khs.go.kr/ENG/contents/E104010000.do) distinguishes those phases.

Choose it when late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century transformation matters more than the founding of Joseon. Its currently published evening hours can also rescue a day when earlier admission elsewhere is impossible. However, Seokjojeon and other exhibition spaces can use reservations or shorter hours than the palace grounds; “the palace is open” does not mean every interior is available.

## Gyeonghuigung: best for learning from what is missing

Gyeonghuigung was a major secondary palace in the late Joseon period, but much of it was dismantled and the surviving site is small compared with its historical footprint. Seoul excavated the area from 1987 and opened restored buildings in 2002. The [Seoul Museum of History's Gyeonghuigung page](https://museum.seoul.go.kr/eng/about/annex/gyeonghuigungPalace.jsp) identifies the restored audience and working halls and publishes the site's current visitor information.

Choose it as a companion to the adjacent museum or as a study of reconstruction and urban erasure. Do not recommend it to a first-time visitor as equivalent to a complete palace complex. Its value is precisely the tension between documentary plan, recovered foundations, moved or restored structures and the modern city.

## Jongmyo: the category check

Jongmyo is often grouped with palace tickets and routes, but it housed royal ancestral tablets and supported state ritual. Its long, restrained shrine buildings, ritual courts and forested setting create a different experience from royal governing and residential spaces. Access also varies between timed guided entry and general-admission days. Use the [Jongmyo field guide](/guides/jongmyo-shrine-first-visit/) before making it the second half of a palace day.

The distinction matters editorially: calling Jongmyo “another palace” erases the function that explains its form.

## Make the final choice in three steps

1. **Choose one historical question.** Founding authority, landscape, royal household, imperial modernity, loss or ancestral ritual are more useful filters than popularity.
2. **Check the operating constraint.** Closure day, last admission, Secret Garden inventory, timed Jongmyo entry and temporary notices can overturn the first choice.
3. **Set a depth limit.** One palace read closely is enough for a half day. If combining two, use our [one-day palace route](/guides/seoul-palace-one-day-route/) and include a real break between them.

The right palace is the one whose differences you will still be able to explain after leaving.
